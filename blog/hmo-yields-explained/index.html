HMO yields explained: gross yield, net yield and cash on cash return

Yield figures get thrown around a lot, and they're often compared unfairly. Here's what each measure really tells you and which one to judge a deal on.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

"This HMO yields 12%." It sounds impressive, but what does it actually mean? Is that before or after costs? On the purchase price or on your cash? Different measures can make the same house look like a brilliant or a mediocre deal.

This guide explains the four numbers that matter and when to use each one.

Gross yield

Gross yield is the simplest measure: yearly rent divided by the property's value or cost.

Gross yield = annual rent ÷ property value × 100

A house worth £500,000 that lets for £60,000 a year has a gross yield of 12%. Gross yield is useful for quickly comparing properties, and it's how many lenders and valuers think about HMO value. But it ignores every cost, and HMOs have more costs than ordinary lets.

Net yield

Net yield takes the running costs off first.

Net yield = (annual rent − running costs) ÷ property value × 100

Running costs for an HMO usually include:

  • bills, if rent is all-inclusive: energy, water, broadband and council tax
  • letting and management fees
  • repairs, maintenance and cleaning of shared areas
  • insurance and licensing
  • an allowance for empty rooms between tenants (voids)

Net yield is far more honest than gross yield, but it still ignores how the deal is funded.

Cash on cash return

For most investors, this is the number that matters. It answers a simple question: how much does each pound of my own money earn each year?

Cash on cash return = annual profit after mortgage ÷ your own cash left in the deal × 100

Here's why it's so useful. Imagine two investors buy identical HMOs. One pays cash. The other uses a mortgage and, after refinancing, leaves only part of their money in. The second investor's profit is smaller in pounds, because of mortgage interest, but much larger as a percentage of their cash, and they have money left over to buy again.

Return on capital employed (ROCE)

ROCE is similar to cash on cash, but some investors include capital growth or mortgage repayment in the return. It's a fair measure over a long period, but be careful when comparing it to other people's figures, because there's no single agreed formula.

Which number should you use?

  • Use gross yield to quickly screen areas and properties.
  • Use net yield to understand how efficient the property is to run.
  • Use cash on cash return to decide whether a deal is a good use of your money.

Watch out for these tricks

  • Yields on purchase price only, which ignore the cost of the conversion.
  • Rents at 100% occupancy, with no allowance for voids.
  • Missing bills, when rents are quoted bills-included.
  • Ignoring tax, which can differ a lot between owning personally and through a company. See HMO tax basics.
