The money

Buying property through a limited company: pros, cons and costs

More and more landlords buy through a company. Here's how it works, how it's taxed and when it does and doesn't make sense.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

Since mortgage interest relief was restricted for individual landlords, buying through a limited company has become the default for many investors, especially those building HMO portfolios. But it isn't right for everyone. This guide explains the main differences.

This is general information, not tax advice. Always speak to a property tax adviser before deciding how to own property.

How it works

You set up a limited company, often a special purpose vehicle (SPV) that exists only to own property. The company buys the property, takes out the mortgage, receives the rent and pays the costs. You're usually a director and shareholder, and lenders will normally ask you for a personal guarantee.

Many lenders prefer SPVs with property-related SIC codes such as 68100 (buying and selling own real estate) or 68209 (letting and operating of own or leased real estate).

The tax difference

Owning personally

Rental profit is added to your other income and taxed at your income tax rate. Under the rules often called Section 24, you can't deduct mortgage interest as an expense. Instead, you get a tax credit worth 20% of the interest. For higher and additional rate taxpayers, that means paying tax on money spent on interest. We explain this in Section 24 explained.

Owning through a company

The company pays corporation tax on its profit, and mortgage interest is fully deductible as a business cost. Corporation tax is 19% on profits up to £50,000 and 25% on profits over £250,000, with marginal relief in between.

The catch is getting money out. If you take profits as dividends, you pay dividend tax personally. Many investors leave profits in the company to fund the next deposit, which is where the structure works best.

Advantages of a company

  • Full deduction of mortgage interest.
  • Lower tax on retained profits for higher-rate taxpayers.
  • Lenders often use a gentler rental stress test for companies.
  • Flexibility for estate planning, for example through different share classes.
  • Easier to bring in partners or other investors.

Disadvantages

  • Mortgage rates can be slightly higher, with fewer products.
  • Running costs: annual accounts, a confirmation statement and accountancy fees.
  • Getting money out means dividend or income tax.
  • Moving existing property into a company usually triggers stamp duty and capital gains tax, as it's treated as a sale at market value. Some partnership arrangements may qualify for reliefs, but these are complex and need specialist advice.
  • Stamp duty: companies pay the 5% surcharge on residential purchases, and a 17% flat rate can apply to residential properties over £500,000 unless a relief applies, such as for a genuine rental business. See stamp duty for investors.

Who usually benefits most

  • Higher and additional rate taxpayers.
  • Investors planning to build a portfolio and reinvest profits.
  • People buying with business partners.
  • Investors with significant mortgage borrowing.

Who might be better owning personally

  • Basic rate taxpayers with small portfolios.
  • Investors who need the rental income to live on.
  • People buying without a mortgage.

Practical steps

  1. Speak to a property tax adviser about your full situation.
  2. Set up the company with suitable SIC codes before you buy.
  3. Open a business bank account.
  4. Use a broker who knows limited company lending.
  5. Keep company and personal money separate.
