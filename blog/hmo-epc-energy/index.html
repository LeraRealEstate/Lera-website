EPC rules for HMOs: getting ready for band C by 2030

Energy efficiency rules for rented homes are getting tougher. Here's what the move to EPC band C means for HMO landlords and how to plan upgrades sensibly.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

Energy Performance Certificates (EPCs) rate a home's energy efficiency from A (best) to G (worst). For landlords in England and Wales, they're tied to the Minimum Energy Efficiency Standards (MEES), which set the lowest rating a rented home can have. Those standards are rising, and HMO investors need to plan for it.

The rules today

Currently, a privately rented home that needs an EPC must have a rating of at least band E, unless a valid exemption is registered. Letting a home below that can lead to penalties.

What's changing: band C by 2030

The government has confirmed that privately rented homes in England will need to reach EPC band C by 1 October 2030. Key points:

  • The requirement applies to all tenancies by the deadline, not just new ones.
  • There's a cost cap of £10,000 per property. If you've spent that much on qualifying improvements and still can't reach C, you can register an exemption.
  • Maximum fines for non-compliance rise to £30,000.
  • The EPC itself is being reformed, with new metrics that look at fabric efficiency, heating systems and running costs more clearly.

Details can change as the rules are finalised, so check the latest government guidance.

Do HMOs need an EPC?

This has long been a grey area. An EPC is needed when a building is sold or let as a whole. Where rooms in a shared house are let individually, an EPC has not always been legally required for each letting. However:

  • most lenders and valuers want to see one
  • many councils expect one as part of licensing
  • HMOs let on a joint tenancy for the whole house definitely need one
  • a good rating helps your marketing and lowers bills, which matters when rent is bills-included

In practice, treat every HMO as needing a good EPC.

Why HMOs often score well, and sometimes don't

A full HMO conversion is a great chance to improve efficiency, as walls and floors are opened up anyway. But some features common in HMOs can pull a rating down:

  • electric showers and electric panel heaters, which can score poorly on cost-based ratings
  • large hot water demand
  • loft rooms with limited insulation space

Plan upgrades as part of the build

The cheapest time to improve efficiency is during the conversion. Insulating a floor that's already up, or fitting a better heating system while the plumbing is being redone, costs far less than coming back later. Ask your assessor for a pre-works EPC and recommendations, then build the upgrades into the design.

Buying with 2030 in mind

When appraising a property, look at its current EPC and the recommendations on it. A house rated F or G may need significant spending to reach C. That doesn't make it a bad deal, but the cost needs to be in your numbers. Our due diligence checklist covers this.

The benefits beyond compliance

  • Lower bills, which in a bills-included HMO means higher profit. See bills-included rent.
  • Warmer, healthier homes that keep tenants longer.
  • Better finance. Some lenders offer green mortgage products for efficient homes.
  • Future-proof value as buyers and lenders pay more attention to efficiency.
