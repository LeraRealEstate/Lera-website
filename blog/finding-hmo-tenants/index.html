Running an HMO

Finding and keeping great HMO tenants

Full rooms and long-staying tenants are the secret to HMO profits. Here's how to advertise, screen fairly and keep people happy under the new rules.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

An HMO's income depends on its rooms being full. Every empty week costs money, and every bad tenancy costs more. The good news is that finding and keeping great tenants is a skill, not luck. This guide covers the process from advert to renewal.

Know who you're letting to

Different HMOs suit different tenants. Before you advertise, be clear about your market:

  • Young professionals: want en-suites, fast Wi-Fi, good transport and a quiet, clean house.
  • Key workers, such as NHS staff near hospitals: value location, flexibility and reliability.
  • Students: let by academic year, often in groups, with very seasonal demand.
  • People on Universal Credit: a large, often under-served market with steady demand. See Universal Credit tenants.

Design, furnishing and price should all match the people you want to attract.

Advertising rooms

  • Use the right platforms. Room-share websites and social media groups reach most HMO tenants. Major property portals work for whole-house lets.
  • Great photos are everything. Bright, wide-angle, professional photos of each room and the shared areas. A short video tour helps too.
  • Write a clear, honest listing. Room size, en-suite or not, what bills are included, transport links and who lives there.
  • Show the rent clearly. Under the Renters' Rights Act, the asking rent must be stated in the advert and you can't invite or accept offers above it.
  • Respond fast. Good tenants view several rooms. The first landlord to reply often wins.

Screening fairly and legally

Since 1 May 2026, the Renters' Rights Act has made it unlawful in England to refuse tenants just because they receive benefits or have children. You can still check whether someone can afford the rent and is likely to be a good tenant, as long as you apply the same checks to everyone. Read the Renters' Rights Act explained.

Typical checks include:

  • Right to rent: a legal requirement in England. Check and copy ID before the tenancy starts.
  • Identity and credit checks.
  • Affordability: income evidence or benefit statements.
  • References from previous landlords or employers.
  • A guarantor where income is lower, if the tenant agrees.

Rent in advance is now limited. You can't ask for rent before the tenancy agreement is signed, and you can't ask for more than one month in advance.

Think about the household

In an HMO, tenants share a home. A short, friendly conversation at the viewing helps you understand whether someone will fit in, such as their working hours and lifestyle. Always make decisions on fair, consistent grounds, never on protected characteristics such as race, religion, sex or disability.

Moving in well

  • a written tenancy agreement and a detailed inventory with photos
  • deposit protected within 30 days, with the prescribed information given
  • gas safety certificate, EICR, EPC and the government's information sheet for tenants
  • a simple house guide: bins, Wi-Fi, how to report repairs, cleaning rota
  • introductions to housemates

Keeping tenants longer

Under the new rules, tenancies are periodic, and tenants can leave with two months' notice. So keeping good tenants is now about giving them reasons to stay:

  • Fix repairs quickly. Slow repairs are the top reason tenants leave.
  • Keep shared areas clean. A regular cleaner for kitchens and bathrooms makes a big difference.
  • Deal with problems between housemates early, calmly and fairly.
  • Keep rent fair. Rent increases now happen once a year through a formal notice, and tenants can challenge them at a tribunal. A fair rent and a happy tenant usually beat a high rent and a void.
  • Small touches: a good mattress, decent Wi-Fi, a welcome pack.

When a room becomes empty

  • Inspect, clean and refresh the room straight away.
  • Re-photograph if it's been improved.
  • Ask current tenants if they know anyone looking. Referrals often make the best housemates.
  • Review the price against local competition.

Dealing with problems

With section 21 abolished, possession now relies on the grounds in Section 8, such as serious rent arrears or anti-social behaviour. Keep clear records of rent, communication and any incidents. Good referencing and early, documented action are your best protection.
