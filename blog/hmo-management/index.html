Running an HMO

Managing an HMO: self-manage or use an agent?

HMOs need more hands-on management than a single let. Here's what the job involves, what agents charge and how to decide which route suits you.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

An HMO is more like a small business than a single buy-to-let. More tenants, more tenancies, more bills, more maintenance and more legal duties. Good management protects your income and your licence. Poor management is the fastest way to turn a great property into a headache.

What HMO management involves

Lettings

  • advertising rooms and arranging viewings
  • referencing tenants and checking right to rent
  • preparing tenancy agreements and inventories
  • registering deposits within 30 days
  • issuing the required documents and information

Day to day

  • collecting rent and chasing arrears
  • handling repairs and emergencies
  • managing utility bills, broadband and council tax where bills are included
  • arranging cleaning of shared areas
  • dealing with disputes between housemates

Compliance

  • gas safety checks every year and an EICR every five years
  • fire alarm testing and servicing, and fire risk assessment reviews
  • regular inspections of shared areas, as required by HMO management regulations
  • licence renewals and conditions

Our HMO compliance calendar lists every check.

Legal duties of an HMO manager

The Management of Houses in Multiple Occupation (England) Regulations 2006 place specific duties on whoever manages an HMO, including:

  • displaying the manager's name, address and phone number in the property
  • keeping fire escapes clear and fire precautions maintained
  • maintaining shared areas, water supply, drainage, gas and electricity
  • providing adequate waste disposal

These apply to all HMOs, licensed or not.

The Renters' Rights Act changes

Since 1 May 2026, the Renters' Rights Act has changed how tenancies work in England. Section 21 "no-fault" evictions are gone, tenancies are periodic, rent can only be increased once a year through a formal process, and rental bidding is banned. A national landlord database is being introduced. Read the Renters' Rights Act explained.

For HMOs, this makes careful tenant selection and good relationships even more important.

Option 1: self-management

Pros

  • no management fee
  • direct control and contact with tenants
  • you learn the business in detail

Cons

  • time-consuming, especially with room turnover
  • you're on call for emergencies
  • keeping up with changing rules is your responsibility
  • hard to scale beyond a few properties

Option 2: a specialist HMO agent

HMO management fees are typically higher than for single lets, often somewhere around 10% to 15% of rent plus VAT, sometimes with letting fees per room. Check exactly what's included.

Pros

  • time freed up for your work, family or next deal
  • experienced in tenant finding and compliance
  • contractors on call
  • easier to scale a portfolio

Cons

  • cost
  • quality varies a lot between agents
  • less direct control

Questions to ask an HMO agent

  • How many HMOs do you manage, and where?
  • What are your average void periods and arrears levels?
  • How often do you inspect, and do you send reports?
  • Who handles out-of-hours emergencies?
  • Do you manage bills, cleaning and broadband?
  • Are you a member of a redress scheme and client money protection scheme? Both are legal requirements for letting agents in England.

Which is right for you?

If you have time, live nearby and enjoy the work, self-management can boost returns. If you're busy, live far away or want to build a portfolio, a good agent is usually worth every penny. Many investors start with an agent, learn how it's done, and decide later.
