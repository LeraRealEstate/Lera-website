Fire safety in HMOs: the 8 things councils look for

Shared houses carry a higher fire risk, so councils expect a higher standard. Here's what that means in practice.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

Shared houses carry a higher fire risk than family homes, so councils expect a higher standard of fire safety. Most follow the national LACORS fire safety guidance, which scales the requirements to the size of the house and the number of storeys.

The eight essentials

  1. Fire doors. 30-minute fire-resisting doors with self-closers on bedrooms and kitchens.
  2. A protected escape route. A hallway and staircase built to hold back fire and smoke long enough for everyone to get out.
  3. Interlinked detection. Mains-wired smoke and heat alarms that sound throughout the house. The grade of system depends on size and layout.
  4. Emergency lighting. Lights the escape route if the power fails, where the layout needs it.
  5. Kitchen safety. Heat detection, a fire blanket and fire-resisting construction around the kitchen.
  6. An easy exit. Thumb-turn locks on escape doors, so no one needs a key to get out.
  7. A fire risk assessment. Required for the shared areas of an HMO, and kept under review.
  8. Signs and servicing. Clear fire action notices, and alarms and lighting serviced and logged.

Alarm grades in plain English

SystemWhat it meansWhere it's often used
Grade D1Mains-wired, interlinked alarms with a sealed backup batteryMany smaller two-storey HMOs
Grade AA panel-based system with call points, installed and serviced to the British StandardLarger HMOs, more storeys, more occupants
LD1, LD2, LD3How much of the house is covered, from escape routes only (LD3) to every room (LD1)Set by the fire risk assessment and council

The right system depends on the size, layout, number of storeys and the council's view. Ask early rather than guessing.

Common mistakes

  • fire doors that don't close fully or have gaps that are too large
  • self-closers removed or wedged open by tenants
  • bikes, bins or furniture blocking the escape route
  • key-operated locks on front doors
  • alarms not tested or logged
  • new holes through fire-resisting walls or ceilings for cables and pipes, left unsealed

Who is responsible?

Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, the "responsible person" for the shared parts of an HMO, usually the landlord or manager, must carry out a fire risk assessment and keep fire precautions in place. The HMO management regulations also require escape routes to be kept clear and fire equipment maintained. See gas, electrical and alarm safety.

Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms

All rented homes in England need a smoke alarm on every storey used as living accommodation, and a carbon monoxide alarm in any room with a fixed combustion appliance, such as a boiler (gas cookers are excluded). Alarms must be working at the start of every tenancy.
