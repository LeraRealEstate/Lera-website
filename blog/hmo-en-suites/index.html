Are en-suites worth it in an HMO? Costs, rents and design tips

En-suite rooms have become the standard in many HMO markets. Here's when they pay off, what they cost and how to fit them well.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

Ten years ago, many HMOs had one or two shared bathrooms. Today, in most competitive markets, tenants expect their own en-suite. Whether to add them is one of the biggest design decisions you'll make.

Why en-suites have become standard

  • Tenants pay more. An en-suite room typically lets for a meaningful premium over a room with a shared bathroom.
  • Rooms let faster, so voids are shorter.
  • Tenants stay longer, because there's less friction with housemates.
  • Valuations can be stronger, especially for larger HMOs valued on income. See how HMOs are valued.
  • Competition. In many cities, new HMOs and co-living schemes all offer en-suites, so older shared-bathroom houses fall behind.

What does an en-suite cost?

A typical HMO en-suite, including the shower, toilet, basin, extraction, waterproofing, tiling, lighting and plumbing, often costs several thousand pounds per room. The biggest variables are:

  • how far the room is from the soil stack
  • whether a macerator or pumped waste is needed
  • whether floors need to be raised for drainage falls
  • the quality of fittings and tiling

Pods (prefabricated bathroom units) can save time on larger projects, but bespoke en-suites usually make better use of awkward spaces.

Does the premium cover the cost?

A simple test. Suppose each en-suite costs £6,000 and adds £60 a month to the room rent:

  • Extra rent: £720 a year
  • Payback from rent alone: just over 8 years

Now add the effect on voids, tenant quality and valuation, and the case is usually much stronger. In weaker markets where the premium is small, a mix of en-suites and shared bathrooms may be smarter.

Space: the hidden cost

An en-suite takes floor area from the bedroom. Many councils don't count en-suite space towards the minimum bedroom size, so the remaining bedroom must still meet the standard. For a single occupant, the national minimum is 6.51 m², and many councils set higher local standards. See HMO room sizes.

A compact en-suite might take around 2.5 to 3 m². So a bedroom needs to be large enough to give that up and still meet the standard with room for a bed, desk and wardrobe.

Design tips

  • Plan the drainage first. Stack en-suites above each other where possible to share waste runs.
  • Use good extraction. Humidity-controlled fans prevent mould and protect the building.
  • Choose electric showers carefully. They remove pressure on the boiler, but they need the right electrical supply.
  • Use wet-room style trays or low-profile trays to save height and look modern.
  • Pick durable, easy-clean finishes. Large-format tiles or wall panels mean fewer grout lines to clean.
  • Think about light. Good lighting and a mirror make small spaces feel bigger in listing photos.

Shared bathrooms: the rules if you don't add en-suites

Councils set minimum ratios of bathrooms and toilets to occupants. A common standard is one bathroom for every five occupants, with an extra separate toilet in some cases, but local rules vary. Wash basins in bedrooms may also be required where there's no en-suite.

Hot water and heating

Six showers running in the morning will overwhelm a standard combi boiler. Options include:

  • a system boiler with a large unvented cylinder
  • electric showers in each en-suite
  • a commercial-grade water heater in larger HMOs

Get a heating engineer to size the system for the full number of tenants.

Maintenance

More bathrooms mean more to maintain. Build in regular checks of seals, extraction fans and drains. Small leaks caught early are cheap. Leaks through a ceiling below are not.
