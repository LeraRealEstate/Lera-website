How HMO finance works: bridging, refinancing and getting your cash back

Why most HMO projects use two kinds of borrowing, what lenders look for, and how a refinance returns your cash.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

Most HMO conversion projects use two kinds of borrowing: a short-term bridging loan to buy and convert the house, then a long-term HMO mortgage once it's finished and let.

Stage 1: the bridging loan

A bridging loan typically covers around 75% of the purchase price, plus the build. Interest is higher than on a mortgage, but it's only for a few months, and some of it can be rolled up and paid at refinance. You fund the deposit, the purchase costs and part of the works.

Stage 2: the HMO mortgage

Once the house is converted, licensed and let, it's revalued as an HMO. A long-term mortgage repays the bridging loan, and because the conversion has added value, the new mortgage can release a large part of your cash. In our worked example, about £116,500 of the investor's money came back at refinance.

The rent test

Lenders check that the rent covers the mortgage interest by a set margin, known as the interest cover ratio. It's typically 125–130% for limited companies and basic-rate taxpayers, and 140–145% for higher-rate taxpayers buying personally.

How the house is valued

Most six-bed HMOs are valued on a bricks and mortar basis, like any other house, against local sales. Larger, fully licensed HMOs can sometimes be valued commercially, on their rental income instead, which can come in 20–40% higher on the right property.

A simple worked example

Illustrative only. An investor buys a house for £400,000 and spends £150,000 converting it into a six-bed HMO:

  • Bridging loan at 75% of the purchase price: £300,000
  • Cash needed for the rest of the price, works, stamp duty and fees: roughly £300,000
  • Finished HMO valued at £750,000
  • HMO mortgage at 75%: £562,500
  • Repay the bridge (£300,000 plus interest and fees), and much of the original cash comes back

The exact amount returned depends on the valuation, costs and rates. Our HMO full calculator runs these numbers for any deal.

The risks to plan for

The refinance is where the plan meets reality. A lower valuation, higher interest rates or a delayed licence can all reduce how much cash comes back. We build contingency into every appraisal and stress-test the numbers before you commit.
