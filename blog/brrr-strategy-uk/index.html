The money

The BRRR strategy explained: buy, refurbish, refinance, rent

BRRR lets investors recycle much of their cash into the next property. Here's how it works with an HMO, where it goes wrong and how to plan it properly.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

BRRR stands for buy, refurbish, refinance, rent (some people add a fifth R for repeat). It's the strategy behind most fast-growing property portfolios, and it's especially powerful with HMOs because a conversion can add a lot of value.

The idea in one paragraph

You buy a property that needs work, usually with short-term finance or cash. You improve it so it's worth more and earns more. You then take out a long-term mortgage based on the new, higher value, which pays back most of what you put in. The property is let, earning income, and you use the money you got back to do it again.

Step 1: Buy

Everything depends on buying well. You want a house where the works will add more value than they cost. For HMOs, that usually means a house with room to add bedrooms through an extension, a loft conversion or reconfiguring the ground floor. Read how to find the right property for what to look for.

Most BRRR purchases are funded with a bridging loan plus your own deposit, because many standard mortgage lenders won't lend on properties that need major work.

Step 2: Refurbish

For an HMO, refurbishment usually includes:

  • adding rooms with a rear extension or loft dormer
  • en-suite bathrooms
  • a kitchen big enough for the number of tenants
  • fire safety: fire doors, alarms and escape routes
  • new electrics, plumbing and heating where needed
  • furnishing every room

The build is where most BRRR plans go wrong. Delays mean more months of bridging interest, and overspends mean more cash left in. A fixed-price contract and an experienced HMO builder are worth paying for.

Step 3: Refinance

Once the works are finished and the house meets HMO standards, you apply for an HMO mortgage. The lender instructs a valuer, who values the property in its new form. Lenders typically lend up to 75% of that value, which pays off the bridging loan and, ideally, returns most of your own money.

Some lenders insist the property is let before they'll refinance. Others will lend on a finished but empty house. Some also apply a minimum ownership period of six months before they'll lend on the new value. Plan this into your timeline from day one.

How the valuer approaches the HMO is critical. Larger HMOs are often valued on their income, like a small commercial investment, which can produce a much higher figure than an ordinary house valuation. We explain this in how HMOs are valued.

Step 4: Rent

With the mortgage in place, the property is let room by room. The rent pays the mortgage, bills and running costs, and what's left is your profit. Good management keeps the rooms full and the tenants happy.

What can go wrong

  • A low valuation, which leaves more of your money in the deal.
  • Interest rates rising between purchase and refinance, which reduces how much lenders will offer.
  • Build overruns, which add cost and bridging interest.
  • Planning or licensing delays, which push back the refinance.

The way to protect yourself is to plan conservatively: assume a slightly lower value, a slightly longer build and slightly higher rates, and only proceed if the deal still works.
