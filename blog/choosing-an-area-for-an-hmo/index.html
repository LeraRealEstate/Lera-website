How to choose an area for an HMO: the demand signals that matter

Room demand isn't spread evenly. These are the signals we use to judge whether an area will keep six rooms full, year after year.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

Ask ten investors where to buy an HMO and you'll get ten different towns. The truth is that location matters less at city level than at street level. A strong HMO area is simply one where lots of people want to rent a room, can afford a fair rent and can get to work easily.

Here's how to judge that properly.

1. Who will live there?

Start by picturing your tenant. Most good HMOs today are let to young working professionals: nurses, junior doctors, engineers, retail and logistics managers, contractors and people new to a city. Some areas suit students, and some suit tenants on Universal Credit. Each group wants different things, so decide who you're building for before you choose the area.

  • Professionals want transport links, nearby shops and gyms, and good quality rooms, ideally with en-suites.
  • Students want to be close to the campus and their friends, and they rent on an academic-year cycle.
  • Tenants on benefits are often more stable but rents are capped by the Local Housing Allowance. We explain this in our article on Universal Credit tenants.

2. Employment within easy reach

The single best predictor of room demand is a large, stable employer within a short commute. Hospitals are a favourite because they employ thousands of shift workers who want to live close by. Universities, airports, logistics hubs, town centres and business parks all play the same role.

Pull up a map, draw a 30-minute travel radius around your target street and list the major employers inside it. If the list is thin, think again.

3. Transport links

Tenants without cars want to walk to a station. As a rough rule, a house within 10 to 15 minutes' walk of a tube, overground or mainline station will let faster and for more money than an identical house a bus ride away.

4. Real room rents, not asking rents

Search SpareRoom and similar sites for your target postcode and note:

  • how many rooms are advertised
  • the asking rents for standard rooms and for rooms with en-suites
  • how long ads stay live, which hints at how fast rooms let
  • what's included: bills, cleaning, broadband

Lots of cheap, tired rooms on the market isn't necessarily bad news. It can mean demand is high but quality is low, which is an opportunity for a well-finished house.

5. The planning and licensing picture

Two areas with identical rents can carry very different risks. In places with an Article 4 direction, any HMO needs planning permission, and some councils also limit how many HMOs can sit in one street. Many councils also run additional licensing schemes that cover smaller HMOs. Check both before you commit. Our Article 4 map and licensing guide are good starting points.

Article 4 isn't always bad. It limits new supply, which can protect rents for existing, well-run HMOs. It simply means you need planning permission in hand, or a house that already has HMO use, before you can let rooms.

6. House prices that leave room for profit

An HMO works when room rents are high relative to house prices. Very expensive areas often fail this test, because a six-bedroom HMO doesn't earn six times the rent of a family home. Areas where ordinary three-bedroom houses are affordable but there's strong demand for rooms usually produce the best yields.

7. The street itself

Once you have an area, walk the streets at different times of day. Look for:

  • well-kept houses and front gardens
  • parking pressure, which affects tenants with cars
  • how many houses already have multiple doorbells or wheelie bins, a sign of HMO density
  • noise from main roads, railways or nightlife

Bringing it together

The best HMO areas combine strong local employment, good transport, healthy room rents and house prices that leave a margin. Areas that tick all four are less common than you might think, which is why preparation pays.
