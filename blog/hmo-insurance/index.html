Running an HMO

HMO insurance: what cover you need and what to watch out for

Standard home insurance won't cover an HMO. Here's what specialist HMO landlord insurance should include, and the common gaps that leave investors exposed.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

Insurance isn't the exciting part of property investing, but it's what protects years of work from a single fire, flood or claim. HMOs carry more risk than a family let: more people, more appliances, more cooking and more chance of something going wrong. Most mainstream policies won't cover them, so you need specialist HMO landlord insurance.

Why ordinary landlord insurance isn't enough

Many standard landlord policies exclude HMOs, or limit cover to a single family or a small number of sharers. If you don't tell your insurer the property is an HMO, they could refuse a claim. Always describe the property accurately: number of tenants, type of tenancies, tenant types and any works planned.

Your mortgage lender will require buildings insurance, and many councils ask for evidence of cover when you apply for an HMO licence.

What a good HMO policy should cover

Buildings insurance

Covers the structure against fire, flood, storm, subsidence and escape of water. Make sure the sum insured is the full rebuild cost, not the market value. Rebuild cost includes demolition, professional fees and meeting current building regulations. A professional rebuild valuation is worth doing for larger HMOs.

Landlord's contents

HMOs are usually furnished, so cover the furniture, white goods and fittings you provide in rooms and shared areas. Tenants should insure their own belongings.

Loss of rent

If a fire or flood makes the property uninhabitable, loss of rent cover pays the rent you'd have received while it's repaired. Check the limit, usually expressed as a percentage of the sum insured or a number of months, and make sure it reflects the full room-by-room rent.

Property owner's liability

Protects you if a tenant or visitor is injured because of the property's condition. Cover of £2 million to £5 million or more is typical. With more people living in an HMO, this matters.

Employer's liability

If you employ anyone, such as a cleaner or handyman, you may legally need employer's liability insurance.

Legal expenses

Helps with legal costs for disputes, including possession claims. With section 21 abolished, possession claims now go through the courts on specific grounds, so legal cover can be valuable. Check what's included.

Optional extras

  • Accidental damage, which isn't always standard.
  • Malicious damage by tenants, which many policies exclude unless added.
  • Rent guarantee insurance, though it's harder to find for room lets and usually needs strict referencing.
  • Trace and access to find the source of hidden leaks.
  • Alternative accommodation for tenants if the property becomes uninhabitable.

The renovation gap

Many standard policies limit or stop cover if a property is unoccupied for more than 30 or 60 days, or if it's undergoing major works. During an HMO conversion, you need specific unoccupied or renovation insurance. Your builder should also have their own contractor's all-risks and public liability insurance. Ask to see their certificates before work starts.

Common reasons claims fail

  • not telling the insurer it's an HMO, or how many tenants live there
  • under-insuring the rebuild cost
  • leaving the property empty longer than the policy allows
  • failing to meet policy conditions, such as keeping gas and electrical certificates current or testing fire alarms
  • gradual damage, such as long-term leaks, which policies often exclude

Keeping premiums down

  • good fire safety: interlinked alarms, fire doors and an up-to-date fire risk assessment
  • modern wiring and a current EICR
  • regular inspections and quick repairs
  • a higher voluntary excess, if you can afford it
  • using a specialist broker who works with HMO insurers

Review every year

Rebuild costs and rents change. At each renewal, check the sum insured, the loss of rent limit and whether anything about the property has changed, such as an extra room or a new type of tenant.
