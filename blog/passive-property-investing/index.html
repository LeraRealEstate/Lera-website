Passive property investing in the UK: your options compared

Want property income without becoming a full-time landlord? Here's an honest comparison of the main ways to invest passively in UK property.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

Many people want the long-term benefits of property, such as rental income and growth in value, without the time and stress of finding deals, managing builders and dealing with tenants. There are several ways to invest more passively. Each has different levels of control, risk, cost and return.

This is general information, not financial advice. The value of investments can go down as well as up.

1. Property funds and REITs

Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) and property funds pool money from many investors to own large portfolios of property. You buy shares through a stocks and shares account or ISA.

  • Pros: fully passive, small amounts possible, easy to buy and sell, diversified.
  • Cons: no control, no direct ownership, share prices can move with the stock market, usually no leverage benefit for you.

2. Property crowdfunding and peer-to-peer lending

Platforms let you invest in a share of a property, or lend to developers for interest.

  • Pros: lower minimums, access to bigger projects.
  • Cons: money can be tied up for years, platform risk, limited control, and returns aren't guaranteed. Some platforms have failed in the past. Check whether the platform is FCA authorised and understand what you actually own.

3. Buying a standard buy-to-let with an agent

You buy a property and pay a letting agent to manage it.

  • Pros: you own the asset, can use a mortgage, and stay fairly hands-off once it's let.
  • Cons: you still need to find the property, arrange finance and refurbishment. Yields on single lets can be modest after costs and tax. See HMO vs buy-to-let.

4. Buying sourced deals

Deal sourcers find properties and charge a fee to pass them on. You then handle the purchase, works and letting, often with recommended contractors.

  • Pros: saves search time.
  • Cons: quality varies hugely, and you still manage everything after the purchase. Sourcers must be registered with a property redress scheme, have anti-money laundering supervision and should hold appropriate insurance. Check before paying any fee.

5. Joint ventures

You provide money and an experienced investor provides the work, and you share the profits.

  • Pros: access to expertise.
  • Cons: you may not own the property directly, and you rely heavily on your partner. Some arrangements may fall under financial regulation. Get legal advice and a clear written agreement.

6. A done-for-you service

A company finds the property, arranges the finance, manages the conversion, lets the rooms and sets up management, while you own the property in your own name or company.

  • Pros: you own the asset outright and keep the rent and growth, with very little time needed from you. You can benefit from leverage and from adding value through the conversion.
  • Cons: fees, and you still need the deposit and works money. You rely on the company's competence, so check their track record carefully.

Questions to ask any provider

  • Who owns the property, and whose name is on the title?
  • What exactly are all the fees, and when are they paid?
  • Can I see completed projects and speak to past clients?
  • Are you registered with a redress scheme and supervised for anti-money laundering?
  • What happens if the project runs over budget or the valuation is low?
  • Who manages the property afterwards?

Is passive investing really passive?

Not completely. You'll still need to make key decisions, review reports and take tax advice. But the right structure can cut the time you spend to a few hours a year, while you still own a real asset.
