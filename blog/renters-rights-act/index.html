The Renters' Rights Act: what it means for HMO investors

The biggest change to renting in a generation is now in force. Here's what changed, and why good landlords have little to fear.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

The Renters' Rights Act became law on 27 October 2025, and its main changes came into force on 1 May 2026. It's the biggest change to renting in England in a generation, and it rewards professional, compliant landlords.

The timeline

WhenWhat
27 October 2025The Act becomes law
1 May 2026Main changes in force: periodic tenancies, no more Section 21, new rent rules
31 July 2026Deadline for court claims based on old Section 21 notices
2026 to 2028Landlord database and ombudsman roll out, with registration becoming mandatory
October 2030EPC C for privately rented homes, alongside a new Decent Homes Standard

Dates for the database, ombudsman and Decent Homes Standard are still subject to government confirmation.

What changed

  • Rolling tenancies: fixed terms are gone. All tenancies are periodic, and tenants can leave with two months' notice.
  • No more Section 21: "no-fault" evictions have ended. Landlords use set grounds, such as selling, moving in or rent arrears.
  • Selling or moving in: possible after the first 12 months of a tenancy, with four months' notice.
  • Rent increases: once a year, through a formal notice with two months' warning. Tenants can challenge it at a tribunal.
  • Fair letting: no rental bidding wars, and no more than one month's rent taken in advance.
  • Pets: landlords can't unreasonably refuse a tenant's request to keep a pet.

Tougher enforcement

Councils can issue civil penalties of up to £7,000 for most one-off breaches and up to £40,000 for serious, repeated or deliberate ones. Rent repayment orders can now reach 24 months' rent, and every landlord will have to join the new ombudsman scheme.

Possession under the new rules

With section 21 gone, landlords must use a specific ground under section 8 to regain possession. Grounds include serious rent arrears, anti-social behaviour, the landlord wanting to sell, and the landlord or a close family member wanting to move in. The selling and moving-in grounds can't usually be used in the first 12 months of a tenancy and need four months' notice. Courts need clear evidence, so good records matter.

Rent increases

Rent can be increased once a year using a formal section 13 notice with at least two months' notice. Tenants can challenge an increase at the First-tier Tribunal, which decides the market rent. Rent review clauses in tenancy agreements no longer work as a way to raise rent.

Pets

Tenants can ask to keep a pet, and landlords must respond within 28 days and can't unreasonably refuse. In an HMO, the views of other tenants and any superior lease terms can be relevant to what's reasonable.

Practical checklist

  • use updated tenancy agreements for periodic tenancies
  • give every tenant the government's information sheet where required
  • advertise a fixed asking rent and don't accept offers above it
  • apply referencing consistently, without blanket bans on benefits or children
  • keep all certificates and licences current
  • prepare to register on the new private rented sector database

For tenant finding under the new rules, read finding and keeping great HMO tenants.

What it means for HMOs

In an HMO each room has its own tenancy, so the new rules apply room by room. Because tenants can leave on two months' notice, good re-letting and realistic allowances for empty rooms matter more than ever. So does paperwork: licences, certificates and correct notices.

Demand for good rooms hasn't gone anywhere. What matters now is well-kept homes, correct paperwork and professional management, all of which come as standard with Lera.
