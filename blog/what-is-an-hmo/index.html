What is an HMO? A plain-English guide for investors

The legal definition, the main types of HMO and why the label matters so much for planning, licensing and lending.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

HMO stands for house in multiple occupation. In everyday terms, it's a home shared by people who aren't one family, the classic "house share". In law, it's defined by the Housing Act 2004, and it's one of the most regulated, and most rewarding, ways to let property.

The three-part test

A property is usually an HMO when all three of these are true:

  • 3 or more people live there as their main home
  • they come from 2 or more households, so not all one family
  • they share a kitchen, bathroom or toilet

What counts as a household?

A family counts as one household: parents, children, grandparents, siblings and other relatives, and couples living together. Every unrelated person is their own household. So four friends sharing a house are four households, while a couple with a child is just one.

Common examples

  • Four friends renting a house together on one joint tenancy: an HMO, because they are four households sharing a kitchen.
  • A couple and one lodger: usually not an HMO. A resident landlord with up to two lodgers is normally exempt.
  • Six professionals each renting a room on their own agreement: a classic HMO, and it needs a mandatory licence.
  • A house split into self-contained flats: the flats themselves aren't HMOs, but the building may be a section 257 HMO if the conversion doesn't meet the 1991 Building Regulations.
  • Student houses: HMOs in the same way as any other shared house, though some university-managed halls are exempt.

Exemptions worth knowing

Some shared living isn't classed as an HMO. Examples include properties managed by councils or housing associations, homes with a resident landlord and no more than two lodgers, two people sharing, and some buildings run by educational establishments or religious communities. The exemptions are narrow, so check the details before relying on one.

Why the label matters

Once a house is an HMO, a whole set of rules switches on. It decides whether you need planning permission, whether you need a council licence (any HMO with 5 or more people from 2 or more households does), which room sizes and fire safety standards apply, and which mortgages you can use. The national HMO management regulations apply to every HMO, licensed or not.

Getting the classification wrong is one of the most expensive mistakes a new investor can make. That's why we check planning and licensing on every house before we put it forward.
