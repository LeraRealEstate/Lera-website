Article 4 directions: can this house become an HMO?

Use classes, permitted development and Article 4 explained, plus the checks to make before you buy.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

Turning a family home into an HMO is a change of use in planning law. Whether it needs planning permission depends on two things: how many people will live there, and where the house is.

Use classes in one minute

  • C3, family home: an ordinary house lived in by one household
  • C4, small HMO: 3 to 6 unrelated people sharing facilities
  • Sui generis, large HMO: 7 or more unrelated people, a class of its own

Changing a C3 house to a C4 small HMO is usually permitted development, so no planning application is needed. A large HMO of 7 or more people always needs planning permission.

What is an Article 4 direction?

A council can make an Article 4 direction to remove the permitted development right to convert a family home into a small HMO. In an Article 4 area, even a 3-person HMO needs planning permission. Many London boroughs have them, and some also limit how many HMOs can be in one street or area.

Article 4 doesn't mean an HMO is impossible, but it does mean an application, a decision and a risk of refusal. Buying in an Article 4 area without checking is one of the most common, and costly, HMO mistakes.

Lawful Development Certificates

Where a conversion doesn't need planning permission, a Lawful Development Certificate from the council confirms the use is lawful. It isn't compulsory, but it reassures lenders and future buyers.

Extensions and dormers

Most of our conversions add space with a rear extension or loft dormer. These may fall under permitted development or need planning permission, depending on their size, the location and the property's use. Building Regulations approval applies either way.

Why councils make Article 4 directions

Councils usually say they want to protect family housing, prevent too many HMOs in one area, and manage issues such as parking, waste and noise. Article 4 directions for HMOs are especially common in university towns and parts of London. Some cover the whole council area, others just certain wards or streets.

Planning applications in Article 4 areas

Many councils use a concentration test, for example refusing a new HMO if more than a set percentage of homes within a certain radius are already HMOs, or if it would sandwich a family home between two HMOs. Others focus on room sizes, amenity space, bins and parking. Reading the council's HMO policy before you buy tells you how likely approval is.

Existing HMOs in Article 4 areas

A house lawfully used as a small HMO before the direction came into force can usually continue. If you're buying an existing HMO in an Article 4 area, ask for proof: a lawful development certificate is best, backed by tenancy agreements, licences and council tax records. Without proof, a lender may refuse to treat it as an HMO.

Our Article 4 map shows the HMO Article 4 areas published to the national planning data for councils across England. Search a postcode to see whether a specific road is covered and when the direction came in. Always confirm with the council before you buy.

Before you buy: a checklist

  1. Check the council's Article 4 map for the exact address
  2. Read the planning history of the house and its neighbours
  3. Look for local HMO policies, such as limits on HMOs per street
  4. Check the licensing scheme that applies
  5. Confirm any extension you're counting on is achievable
