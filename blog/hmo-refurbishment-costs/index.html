How much does an HMO conversion cost? A realistic breakdown

Conversion costs make or break an HMO deal. Here's what goes into the budget, where the money really goes and how to avoid overruns.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

Ask ten builders what an HMO conversion costs and you'll get ten answers. The truth is that it depends on the house, the region, the number of rooms and the finish. But the structure of a good budget is always the same. This guide walks through it.

All figures here are illustrative. Prices vary widely across the UK and change over time.

The main cost headings

1. Strip-out and structural work

Removing old kitchens, bathrooms and finishes, plus any walls being moved or removed. Structural work needs steel beams, an engineer's calculations and building control sign-off.

2. Fire safety

Often underestimated. Typical items include:

  • FD30 fire doors with self-closers and intumescent strips to every bedroom and the kitchen
  • a mains-wired, interlinked fire alarm system, usually Grade D1 or Grade A depending on size
  • emergency lighting in larger HMOs
  • fire-resistant plasterboard to protect escape routes
  • fire blankets and, where required, extinguishers

Read fire safety in HMOs for what councils expect.

3. Electrics

Most older houses need a full or partial rewire to support more occupants, more sockets and en-suite extractor fans. A new consumer unit and an EICR on completion are standard.

4. Plumbing and heating

More bathrooms mean more hot water demand. Many HMOs need a larger combi boiler, a system boiler with an unvented cylinder, or electric showers in en-suites. New soil pipes and drainage runs for en-suites can be a big cost in themselves.

5. En-suites and bathrooms

Usually the single biggest line. Each en-suite needs a shower, toilet, basin, extraction, waterproofing and tiling. See are en-suites worth it?

6. Kitchen

A shared kitchen sized for the number of tenants, with enough cooking, storage and fridge space. Councils publish minimum amenity standards.

7. Finishes and furniture

Flooring, decoration, lighting, and furnishing every bedroom and the shared areas. Good-quality, durable furniture pays for itself in lower replacement costs.

8. Professional fees

  • architect or designer drawings
  • structural engineer
  • building control fees
  • planning or lawful development certificate fees
  • party wall surveyor where needed
  • licensing application fee

9. Contingency

Always add a contingency, typically 10% to 15% of the build cost. Older houses hide surprises: rotten joists, damp, old wiring or drainage problems.

An illustrative budget

For a 3-bed terraced house converted into a 6-bed HMO with en-suites, a budget might look like this:

ItemIllustrative cost
Strip-out and structural£10,000 to £20,000
Loft conversion (if adding rooms)£40,000 to £60,000
Fire safety£8,000 to £15,000
Electrics£8,000 to £14,000
Plumbing and heating£8,000 to £15,000
En-suites (6)£30,000 to £50,000
Kitchen£8,000 to £15,000
Finishes and furniture£15,000 to £25,000
Professional fees£6,000 to £12,000
Contingency10% to 15%

London and the South East are often at the top of these ranges or above them. The North and Midlands can be lower.

Costs that sit outside the build

  • Bridging interest while the works happen. See bridging loans explained.
  • Council tax and utilities during the build.
  • Insurance for an empty property under renovation.
  • Letting costs, photography and marketing once finished.

Why projects go over budget

  • No detailed specification, so quotes aren't comparable and extras creep in.
  • Changing the design mid-build.
  • Hidden defects found after the strip-out.
  • Delays that add bridging interest and holding costs.
  • Building control or licensing requirements missed at design stage.

How to control costs

  • Get a full survey before you buy, and have a builder walk the property.
  • Fix the design and specification before work starts.
  • Use fixed-price contracts with clear stage payments.
  • Check the council's HMO standards at design stage, not at the licence inspection.
  • Track spending weekly against the budget.
