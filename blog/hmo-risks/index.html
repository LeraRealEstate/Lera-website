The real risks of HMO investing, and how to manage them

HMOs can produce strong returns, but they carry real risks. Here's an honest look at what can go wrong and how experienced investors protect themselves.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

HMOs can earn much more than a standard buy-to-let. But higher returns come with higher risk and more work. Anyone who tells you HMOs are risk-free isn't being straight with you. This guide sets out the main risks honestly, with the ways investors manage them.

The value of property can go down as well as up. Capital at risk.

1. Regulation and compliance

HMOs are one of the most regulated parts of the rental market: licensing, planning, fire safety, room sizes, amenity standards and the Renters' Rights Act. Rules change regularly, and penalties for getting it wrong are serious.

How to manage it: check planning and licensing before you buy, design to the council's standards, use experienced professionals and keep a compliance calendar. See HMO licensing explained.

2. Planning and Article 4

If a council introduces an Article 4 direction, or refuses planning for a large HMO, your plan may not work. Buying a house you can't legally convert is one of the costliest mistakes.

How to manage it: check every address on our Article 4 map, get planning advice early and make offers conditional where possible.

3. Build cost and delays

Conversions often uncover hidden problems. Overruns and delays eat into profit, and add bridging interest.

How to manage it: a full survey, a detailed specification, fixed-price contracts, a 10% to 15% contingency and close project management. See HMO conversion costs.

4. Valuation and refinance

If the finished property is valued lower than expected, you may leave more money in the deal than planned. Lender criteria and interest rates can also change during a project.

How to manage it: base your plan on a cautious end value, understand how HMOs are valued, and have a cash buffer.

5. Interest rates

Higher rates increase mortgage costs and can reduce how much lenders will lend.

How to manage it: stress-test your numbers at higher rates, consider fixed-rate mortgages and avoid borrowing to the limit.

6. Voids and tenant demand

An HMO relies on rooms being full. Too many HMOs in one area, a big employer closing or a new co-living scheme nearby can affect demand.

How to manage it: research demand properly before buying, build quality rooms that stand out, and price fairly. See choosing an area.

7. Management intensity

More tenants means more lettings, more repairs and more disputes. Poor management leads to voids, damage and compliance problems.

How to manage it: use a specialist HMO manager, or make sure you have the time and skills to do it well. See managing an HMO.

8. Running costs

With bills-included rent, rising energy prices come out of your profit.

How to manage it: efficient buildings, smart controls, fair-use clauses and annual reviews. See bills-included rent.

9. Liquidity and exit

Property takes time to sell. Large HMOs appeal to a smaller pool of buyers than family homes, and some may need converting back to sell to owner-occupiers.

How to manage it: plan your exit from the start and keep the property in excellent condition. See HMO exit strategies.

10. Tax changes

Tax rules for landlords have changed several times in recent years, including mortgage interest relief, stamp duty surcharges and planned property income tax rates.

How to manage it: take specialist tax advice, choose the right ownership structure, and don't rely on today's rules lasting forever.

11. Concentration

Putting all your money into one property in one town is a concentrated bet.

How to manage it: think about your wider finances, keep emergency savings and only invest money you won't need at short notice.

Risk is manageable, not avoidable

None of these risks is a reason to avoid HMOs. They're reasons to approach them properly, with good research, cautious numbers and experienced people. Many of the biggest risks are decided before you buy, which is why due diligence matters so much. See our due diligence checklist.
