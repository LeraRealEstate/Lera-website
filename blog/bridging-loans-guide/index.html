Bridging loans for property investors: a plain-English guide

Bridging finance lets you buy a house that needs work, fast. Here's how it's priced, what it really costs and how to plan your exit.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

A bridging loan is short-term finance secured on property. Investors use it to buy houses that a normal mortgage lender won't touch yet, often because they need major work, and to move quickly when a good deal appears.

For HMO conversions, bridging is often the tool that makes the whole strategy possible. But it's expensive if it runs on too long, so it needs careful planning.

How bridging loans work

  • Term: usually 6 to 18 months.
  • Loan size: commonly up to 70% to 75% of the property's current value. Some lenders also fund part or all of the works.
  • Interest: charged monthly. Rates vary with the market and the deal, and are often in the region of 0.6% to 1% a month.
  • Repayment: the whole loan is repaid at the end, usually from a long-term mortgage (refinance) or a sale. This is called your exit.

Rolled-up, retained or serviced interest

Most investors don't pay bridging interest monthly. Instead, it's either:

  • Rolled up: added to the loan and paid at the end, or
  • Retained: taken from the loan upfront to cover a set number of months.

Either way, the interest is a real cost of the deal, and every extra month adds to it.

The fees people forget

CostTypical amount
Arrangement feeOften around 2% of the loan
Exit feeSome lenders charge around 1%; others charge none
Valuation feeDepends on property value
Lender's legal feesYou usually pay these as well as your own
Broker feeVaries

When you compare bridging offers, look at the total cost over the expected term, not just the monthly rate.

A simple example

Say you borrow £350,000 at 0.85% a month for 6 months, with a 2% arrangement fee and a 1% exit fee:

  • Interest: £350,000 × 0.85% × 6 = £17,850
  • Arrangement fee: £7,000
  • Exit fee: £3,500
  • Total: about £28,350, plus valuation and legal costs

If the project runs 3 months late, that's roughly another £9,000 in interest. That's why planning the timeline matters so much.

Regulated or unregulated?

If you, or a family member, will live in the property, the loan is usually regulated by the FCA. Investment property bought to let is normally unregulated, which gives lenders more flexibility but less consumer protection. Use a broker who understands the difference.

Plan your exit before you borrow

Lenders want to know exactly how you'll repay. For an HMO, the exit is usually an HMO mortgage once the works are finished. Before you take a bridging loan, check:

  • which lenders will refinance the finished HMO, and at what loan to value
  • whether they have a minimum ownership period
  • whether they need the property let first
  • what the property is realistically worth when finished

If the refinance falls short, you may need to put more cash in to clear the bridge, so keep a buffer.

When bridging makes sense

  • Buying a property that's unmortgageable in its current state
  • Buying at auction, where you usually need to complete within 28 days
  • Moving quickly to secure a discount from a motivated seller
  • Funding major works before a refinance at the higher value
