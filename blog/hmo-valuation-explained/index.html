How HMOs are valued: bricks and mortar vs investment value

The same house can be worth very different amounts depending on how the valuer approaches it. Here's why, and how to give your HMO the best chance of a strong valuation.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

When you refinance an HMO, the valuation decides how much you can borrow and therefore how much of your own money you get back. Understanding how valuers think is one of the most useful things an HMO investor can learn.

Two ways to value an HMO

1. Bricks and mortar value

This is how most homes are valued. The surveyor compares the property to similar houses that have sold nearby, adjusting for size, condition and location. The fact that it's an HMO earning a high rent is largely ignored. A six-bedroom HMO might be valued like a large family house on the same street.

2. Investment (commercial) value

Here, the valuer treats the HMO like a small commercial investment and values it on its income. In simple terms, they look at the net rent and apply a yield that reflects what investors would pay for that income in that area.

Investment value ≈ net annual income ÷ investment yield

For example, if a large HMO produces £60,000 a year of net income and the valuer applies a 7.5% yield, the investment value is £800,000, which may be well above the bricks and mortar value.

Which method will the valuer use?

It depends on the lender and the property:

  • Smaller HMOs (often 3 to 5 bedrooms) are usually valued on bricks and mortar.
  • Larger HMOs (often 6 or more rooms, sometimes with planning as sui generis) are more likely to be valued on investment value.
  • Lender policy matters a lot. Some lenders always use bricks and mortar. Others will consider investment value for larger, well-evidenced HMOs.

Choosing the right lender for your property is one of the biggest levers you have, which is why a specialist broker is so valuable.

What drives a strong investment valuation

  • Evidence of rent. Signed tenancy agreements and a rent schedule beat estimates every time.
  • Comparable evidence. Sales of similar HMOs nearby help the valuer justify the yield.
  • Quality and compliance. A licensed, well-finished HMO with en-suites and good amenities feels lower risk, which supports a lower yield and a higher value.
  • Planning certainty. Planning permission or a lawful development certificate for the HMO use removes a major risk.
  • Location and demand. Strong local demand for rooms supports the income the valuer is relying on.

How valuers treat costs

Valuers don't use the gross rent. They deduct running costs such as bills, management, maintenance and voids. If your rents are bills-included, be ready to show realistic bill costs. Overstating income only leads to a down-valuation and lost credibility.

Yields vary by area

The yield a valuer applies isn't fixed. It reflects local evidence and the risk of the investment. Areas with more transactions and stronger demand usually support lower yields and higher values. Our HMO full calculator uses typical yields by area to estimate an end value, but a real valuation can differ.

Preparing for the valuation visit

  • Have the property finished, clean and, ideally, fully let.
  • Prepare a pack: licence, planning documents, certificates, floor plans, tenancy schedule and photos.
  • Include a schedule of the works done and their cost.
  • Be present or have your agent there to answer questions.

When the valuation comes in low

It happens. Options include asking the lender to review it with extra comparable evidence, trying a different lender with a different valuation approach, or waiting until the property has a longer rental track record. Planning your deal with a cautious end value means a low valuation is disappointing rather than disastrous.
