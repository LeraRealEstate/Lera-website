Rules and regulations

Do you need planning permission for an HMO? C3, C4 and sui generis

Whether you need planning to create an HMO depends on its size and where it is. Here's how use classes, permitted development and certificates work.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

Planning and licensing are two different things. A licence is about how an HMO is run and managed. Planning is about whether the building can be used as an HMO at all. Many investors get the licence right but forget the planning, which can be an expensive mistake.

This guide covers England. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have different rules.

The three use classes you need to know

Use classWhat it covers
C3A dwelling house used by a single household, such as a family
C4A small HMO with 3 to 6 unrelated people sharing basic amenities
Sui generis"Of its own kind". Large HMOs with 7 or more people

C3 to C4: usually permitted development

National rules allow a house to change from C3 to C4 without a planning application. This is called permitted development. So in most of England, you can turn a family house into a small HMO for up to 6 people without applying for planning, as long as you meet licensing and building rules.

The exception is where the council has made an Article 4 direction. That removes the permitted development right, so you need full planning permission even for a small HMO. Check any address on our Article 4 map, and read Article 4 explained.

7 or more people: always planning permission

An HMO for 7 or more people is sui generis. There's no permitted development right to create one, so you always need full planning permission, wherever the property is.

Councils look at things like:

  • the concentration of HMOs nearby, often using a percentage threshold within a set radius
  • room sizes and the amount of shared space
  • bin storage, bike storage and parking
  • noise and the effect on neighbours
  • the loss of family housing

Lawful development certificates

If you believe a change of use doesn't need planning, you can apply for a lawful development certificate (LDC). It's formal proof from the council that the use is lawful. Lenders, valuers and future buyers often ask for one, so it's worth having even where permitted development applies.

There are two kinds:

  • Proposed use: before you convert, confirming the change would be lawful.
  • Existing use: proving a use has become lawful, for example because it's been in place for a long time without enforcement. The time limit for most changes of use is now 10 years.

Timing matters with Article 4

Councils usually give at least 12 months' notice before an Article 4 direction comes into force. If a property was lawfully used as a C4 HMO before that date, it can usually carry on. Keep evidence such as tenancy agreements, licences and council tax records. An existing-use LDC is the strongest proof.

Building regulations are separate

Even when planning isn't needed, works to create an HMO usually need building regulations approval. This covers fire doors, alarms, escape routes, structural work, drainage for new bathrooms and electrics. Your building control body will inspect and issue a completion certificate.

What happens if you get it wrong

  • The council can issue an enforcement notice requiring you to stop the HMO use.
  • Lenders may refuse to refinance, which can trap money in a bridging loan.
  • Valuations can fall sharply if the HMO use isn't lawful.
  • A future sale can be delayed or fall through.

Tips for a smooth planning application

  • Check the council's HMO policy and any supplementary planning documents first.
  • Count existing HMOs nearby against the council's threshold.
  • Design generous rooms and shared space. It helps the application and the rent.
  • Show bins, bikes and management arrangements clearly on the plans.
  • Use a planning consultant who knows the council.
