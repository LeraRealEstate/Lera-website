Finding deals

The HMO due diligence checklist: 40 things to check before you buy

Most HMO mistakes are made before the purchase completes. Use this checklist to check the property, the planning, the numbers and the area properly.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

The best time to avoid a bad HMO deal is before you buy it. Once you've exchanged contracts, problems become expensive. This checklist sets out what experienced investors check, grouped into six areas. Not every point applies to every property, but every point is worth asking.

1. Planning and legal use

  • Is the property in an Article 4 area? Check our Article 4 map and the council's website.
  • If it's already an HMO, is the use lawful? Ask for planning permission or a lawful development certificate.
  • For 7 or more occupants, is planning permission realistic given the council's HMO policy and local concentration?
  • Is the property in a conservation area or listed?
  • Are there restrictive covenants on the title, such as "single private dwelling only"?
  • If it's leasehold, does the lease allow HMO use and subletting?
  • Any planning history, refusals or enforcement action on the property?

2. Licensing

  • Will it need a mandatory, additional or selective licence? See HMO licensing explained.
  • What are the council's room size and amenity standards?
  • If licensed already, is the licence transferable? (Licences aren't usually transferable. You'll normally need to apply in your own name.)
  • How long does the council typically take to process licences, and what's the fee?

3. The building

  • Get a full building survey, not just a mortgage valuation.
  • Structure: cracks, movement, roof condition, chimney stacks.
  • Damp, rot and timber problems.
  • Age and condition of the wiring and consumer unit.
  • Boiler age and capacity for more occupants.
  • Drainage: is there a CCTV drain survey? Old clay pipes and shared drains can cause problems.
  • Loft: headroom and potential for conversion.
  • Layout: can rooms meet minimum sizes with en-suites?
  • Current EPC rating and the cost of reaching band C. See EPC rules for HMOs.
  • Japanese knotweed, asbestos (common in homes built before 2000) and flood risk.

4. The area and demand

  • Who will rent here: professionals, students, key workers, people on benefits?
  • How many rooms are advertised nearby, and how long do they stay empty?
  • Achieved room rents for similar quality rooms, not just asking prices.
  • Transport links, employers, hospitals and universities nearby.
  • Planned developments, including new HMOs or co-living schemes.
  • Crime levels and the feel of the street at different times of day.
  • Local Housing Allowance rates, if letting to people on benefits.

5. The numbers

  • Purchase price and stamp duty, including the 5% surcharge. See stamp duty for investors.
  • Legal, survey and finance fees.
  • A detailed works budget with a 10% to 15% contingency.
  • Bridging interest for a realistic timeline, plus a few months' buffer.
  • Realistic rents per room and a void allowance.
  • Running costs: bills, council tax, insurance, management, maintenance, compliance.
  • A cautious end value, and the refinance amount at 75% of that value.
  • Monthly cash flow after the refinance, stress-tested at higher interest rates.
  • How much money is left in the deal, and the return on that money.

6. Finance and exit

  • Will a bridging lender fund it, and will an HMO lender refinance it? See HMO mortgages.
  • Are there minimum ownership periods before refinancing?
  • What's the exit if things change? Could it be sold as an HMO, or converted back to a family house? See exit strategies.

Red flags

  • A seller who won't allow surveys or access for builders.
  • An existing HMO with no paperwork proving lawful use.
  • Rents that only work at the very top of the market.
  • A deal that only works if everything goes perfectly.
  • Pressure to commit without time to check.

Walk away if you need to

The most profitable deal is sometimes the one you don't do. Experienced investors look at many properties for each one they buy. If the checks raise problems you can't price in, move on.
