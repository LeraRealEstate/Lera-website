Rules and regulations

HMO licensing explained: mandatory, additional and selective

Which HMOs need a licence, what the council checks, and what happens if you get it wrong.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

Most HMOs need a licence from the local council. Licensing is how councils make sure shared homes are safe, well managed and not overcrowded. It also matters to lenders: none will complete an HMO mortgage without evidence of the licence.

The three types of licence

Mandatory licensing: everywhere in England

Any HMO with 5 or more people from 2 or more households needs a licence. These national rules were widened to their current form in October 2018, when the old "three storeys or more" requirement was dropped.

Additional licensing: council by council

Councils can extend licensing to smaller HMOs, such as houses with 3 or 4 sharers, across a whole borough or in particular areas. These schemes are common in London.

Selective licensing: council by council

Some councils license every privately rented home in an area, whether or not it's an HMO.

What the council checks

  • Suitability: room sizes, kitchens, bathrooms and toilets are right for the number of people
  • Safety: fire precautions, gas and electrical certificates, alarms and safe furniture
  • Management: proper arrangements to run the house, deal with repairs and look after tenants
  • Fit and proper: the licence holder and manager have no relevant convictions or housing breaches

Duration, conditions and fees

A licence lasts up to five years and then must be renewed, and councils can grant shorter ones. Every licence carries mandatory conditions, and councils can add their own. Fees are set by each council, usually per licence or per room.

How long an application takes varies from weeks to several months. It isn't an offence to let while a properly made application is being processed, so it pays to apply as early as possible.

The application, step by step

  1. Check the scheme. Mandatory, additional or selective, and the council's local standards.
  2. Prepare documents. Floor plans with room sizes, gas safety record, EICR, fire alarm and emergency lighting certificates, a fire risk assessment, tenancy agreement template and proof of insurance are commonly requested.
  3. Apply and pay. Most councils take applications online. Some split the fee into two parts.
  4. Inspection. An officer may visit to check the property matches the application and meets the standards.
  5. Draft licence. You get a draft with conditions and a chance to comment.
  6. Final licence. Issued with the maximum number of occupants and households, and any works to complete by a deadline.

Typical licence conditions

  • providing gas and electrical certificates to the council on request
  • keeping smoke and fire alarms in working order and testing them
  • giving tenants a written statement of terms
  • dealing with anti-social behaviour and keeping records
  • keeping bins and gardens tidy
  • not exceeding the permitted number of occupants

Licensing sits alongside planning, not instead of it. You may need both. See HMO planning explained and amenity standards.

Getting it wrong is expensive

  • an unlimited fine if prosecuted for running an unlicensed HMO, or a civil penalty instead
  • rent repayment orders of up to 24 months' rent, up from 12 under the Renters' Rights Act
  • banning orders that can stop a landlord letting or managing property at all
  • no mortgage: lenders won't complete without the licence
