Co-living vs HMOs: what's the difference and which is better?

Co-living is the big-city, branded cousin of the HMO. Here's how the two compare for tenants and investors, and where each makes sense.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

"Co-living" has become a popular term in the UK property world. Large, purpose-built schemes in cities like London and Manchester offer furnished rooms or studios with shared lounges, gyms and events. But at its heart, co-living is a form of shared living, just like a traditional HMO. So what's really different?

What is an HMO?

A house in multiple occupation is a property where three or more unrelated people share facilities such as a kitchen or bathroom. Most HMOs are converted houses with 4 to 8 rooms, often with en-suites, a shared kitchen and a living area. Read what is an HMO?

What is co-living?

Co-living usually means larger, professionally managed buildings, often purpose-built, with:

  • private studios or en-suite rooms, sometimes with a small kitchenette
  • generous shared spaces: lounges, co-working areas, gyms, roof terraces
  • a strong brand, community events and app-based management
  • all-inclusive pricing and flexible tenancy lengths

In planning terms, large co-living schemes are usually treated as "large-scale purpose-built shared living" (sui generis), with their own planning policies, especially in London.

What tenants like about each

HMOs appeal to people who want an affordable, homely place in a residential street, with a small group of housemates. Co-living appeals to people who want hotel-style convenience, extra facilities and a big social scene, and are willing to pay more for it.

For investors: which is better?

For individual investors, the choice is usually simple. Large co-living schemes cost tens of millions of pounds to build and are mostly funded by institutions. The realistic way for an individual to access shared living is through HMOs.

However, the best HMOs borrow ideas from co-living:

  • en-suites to every room
  • stylish, well-designed shared spaces
  • fast, reliable Wi-Fi
  • all-inclusive bills and simple pricing
  • professional management with easy repair reporting
  • a cleaner for shared areas

Does co-living compete with HMOs?

In some city centres, yes, especially for young professionals at the top of the market. But co-living is usually more expensive, so a high-quality HMO at a lower price can be very competitive. In most towns and suburbs, co-living isn't present at all.

When researching an area, check for new co-living or purpose-built student schemes that could affect demand. See our due diligence checklist.

Rules for both

Both HMOs and co-living must meet fire safety and housing standards. Smaller HMOs fall under HMO licensing, while large co-living buildings are often regulated through planning conditions, building safety rules and management plans. The Renters' Rights Act changes to tenancies apply to most assured tenancies in both.

The bottom line

Co-living has shown that tenants will pay for quality, design and convenience in shared living. For individual investors, a well-designed HMO is the practical way to tap into that demand, with a real asset you own.
