Running an HMO

Council tax for HMOs: who pays and how it's banded

Council tax works differently in HMOs. Here's who is liable, how properties are banded, the discounts and exemptions, and what happens while a house is empty.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

Council tax can be a surprise for new HMO investors. In a normal let, the tenant pays. In most HMOs, the owner does. This guide explains the rules in England and how to plan for them.

Who is liable in an HMO?

Council tax rules make the owner liable for properties that are HMOs, rather than the tenants. This applies to most shared houses where tenants rent rooms on separate agreements. Because of this, most HMO landlords include council tax in an all-inclusive rent. See bills-included rent.

Where a group of sharers rents the whole house on a single joint tenancy, liability can sit with the tenants instead. Check with your council how they treat your property.

How HMOs are banded

Normally, an HMO is valued as a single dwelling and placed in one council tax band by the Valuation Office Agency (VOA). In the past, some HMOs, particularly those with rooms that had their own kitchen facilities, were split into several separately banded units, known as disaggregation. This sharply increased bills.

The rules were changed in England in 2023 so that, in most cases, a shared house is again treated as one property. Rooms that are genuinely self-contained, with their own kitchen and bathroom behind a lockable door, may still be banded separately. If you're adding kitchenettes to rooms, check the council tax effect first.

Discounts and exemptions

  • All students: a property occupied only by full-time students is exempt.
  • Some students: if a house has students and non-students, the exemption doesn't apply, though discounts may.
  • Disabled band reduction can apply where a property has been adapted for a disabled resident.
  • Local discretionary reductions vary between councils.

Council Tax Reduction for people on low incomes is usually linked to the person who is liable. Where the owner is liable, tenants generally can't claim it for that property.

Empty homes and refurbishments

While you're converting a house, it may be empty for months. Rules vary by council, but:

  • many councils no longer give any discount for empty or unfurnished properties
  • since April 2024, councils in England can charge a 100% premium (double council tax) on homes empty and unfurnished for more than one year, with higher premiums for longer periods
  • some councils offer a discount for homes undergoing major structural repair

A typical HMO conversion takes a few months, so the long-term premium shouldn't usually apply, but include council tax in your project budget.

Budgeting council tax

Council tax depends on the band and the council, and it usually rises every April. When appraising a deal:

  • check the current band on the government's council tax band search
  • look up the council's current charges for that band
  • allow for an annual increase
  • remember that adding rooms or extending won't change the band until the property is sold or revalued, but separately banded rooms can

Challenging your band

If you think your band is wrong, you can ask the VOA to review it. Be careful: a review can put a band up as well as down. Compare similar homes on your street before you ask.

Practical tips

  • Tell the council when the property becomes an HMO and confirm who is liable in writing.
  • Keep records of tenancy types and dates.
  • Pay by direct debit so you never fall into arrears.
  • If tenants are students, collect student certificates each year to keep the exemption.
