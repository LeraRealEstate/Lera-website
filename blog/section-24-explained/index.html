Section 24 explained: how mortgage interest is taxed for landlords

Section 24 changed how individual landlords get tax relief on mortgage interest. Here's how it works, who it hits hardest and what investors do about it.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

"Section 24" is the name landlords use for the rules that restrict tax relief on mortgage interest for residential property owned by individuals. It was phased in between April 2017 and April 2020, and it changed the maths of buy-to-let for many investors.

This is general information, not tax advice. Tax rules change, so always check the latest position with a property tax adviser.

What changed

Before Section 24, an individual landlord could deduct all of their mortgage interest from their rental income before working out the tax. Now, they can't. Instead:

  • Tax is worked out on rental profit before mortgage interest.
  • The landlord then gets a tax credit worth 20% of the interest.

For a basic rate taxpayer, the result is roughly the same as before. For higher and additional rate taxpayers, it means paying tax on money that went straight to the lender.

A worked example

Imagine a higher-rate taxpayer with £30,000 of rent, £6,000 of running costs and £14,000 of mortgage interest.

Old rulesSection 24
Rent£30,000£30,000
Running costs−£6,000−£6,000
Mortgage interest deducted−£14,000£0
Taxable profit£10,000£24,000
Tax at 40%£4,000£9,600
20% interest credit£0−£2,800
Tax to pay£4,000£6,800

The real cash profit is £10,000 in both cases, but the tax bill rises by £2,800. That's a tax rate of 68% on the actual profit.

The hidden effect: being pushed into a higher band

Because the interest is no longer deducted, your taxable income looks higher. That can push a basic rate taxpayer into the higher rate band. It can also affect other things linked to income, such as the High Income Child Benefit Charge or the loss of your personal allowance once income passes £100,000.

What's coming next

In the November 2025 Budget, the government announced separate tax rates for property income, planned to be 2 percentage points higher than the standard rates from April 2027. The way finance costs are relieved is also being adjusted alongside this change. If you own property personally, ask your adviser how the new rates affect your figures.

Who Section 24 does not apply to

  • Limited companies. Companies deduct mortgage interest in full and pay corporation tax on the profit. See buying through a limited company.
  • Furnished holiday lets used to be exempt, but that special regime was abolished from April 2025.
  • Commercial property is not affected.

How investors respond

  • Buying new properties through a company, so interest is fully deductible.
  • Using lower loan-to-value borrowing on personally owned property, to reduce interest.
  • Choosing higher-yielding strategies such as HMOs, where rent covers interest more comfortably.
  • Splitting ownership with a spouse or partner on a lower tax band, where that suits the family. This needs proper advice and paperwork.
  • Selling weaker properties and reinvesting in fewer, stronger ones.

Should you move existing properties into a company?

Usually, transferring a property you already own into a company counts as a sale at market value. That can trigger capital gains tax and stamp duty. Some investors with genuine property businesses explore partnership routes and incorporation relief, but this is a specialist area and HMRC looks at it closely. Don't do it without expert advice.

Why HMOs cope better with Section 24

An HMO typically earns far more rent than the same house let as a single unit, while the mortgage is similar. Interest is a smaller share of the rent, so the tax effect is less painful. Our article on HMO vs buy-to-let shows the difference.
