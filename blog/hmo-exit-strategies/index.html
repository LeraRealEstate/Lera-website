HMO exit strategies: hold, refinance, sell or convert back

Every good property plan includes a way out. Here are the main exit options for HMO investors, and how to keep them open from day one.

Lera Real Estate · Updated 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

Most HMO investors plan to hold for the long term. But life changes, markets change and rules change. A good investor plans their exit before they buy, so they're never forced into a bad decision. This guide covers the main options.

1. Hold for the long term

For many investors, the plan is to keep the HMO for decades, collecting rent and benefiting from growth in value. Over time, the mortgage becomes smaller relative to the property's value and rent usually rises.

To hold well, you need good management, regular upgrades to keep rooms competitive, and an eye on future rules such as EPC targets. See EPC rules for HMOs.

2. Refinance to release equity

As values rise, you can remortgage to release some of the increase, often to fund your next deposit. This lets you grow a portfolio without selling.

Points to watch:

  • more borrowing means higher mortgage costs and less cash flow
  • lenders will stress-test the rent against the new loan
  • borrowing too heavily leaves less room if rents fall or rates rise

Read the BRRR strategy explained for how refinancing fits into growth.

3. Sell as an HMO to another investor

A well-run, fully let, licensed HMO with planning in place is an attractive product for other investors. Larger HMOs valued on investment value may sell on their income, which can be well above bricks and mortar value. See how HMOs are valued.

What buyers will want to see:

  • planning permission or a lawful development certificate
  • the licence and its conditions
  • a tenancy schedule with rents and start dates
  • running costs and bills over the last year or two
  • certificates and the fire risk assessment

Under the Renters' Rights Act, you can no longer use section 21 to end tenancies before a sale. There is a new possession ground for landlords who intend to sell, but there are restrictions, including on using it early in a tenancy. In practice, many HMOs will be sold with tenants in place, which suits investor buyers.

4. Convert back to a family home

A small C4 HMO can usually change back to a C3 family home without planning permission. That can open up the much bigger owner-occupier market, which may pay more in some areas, especially where family homes are in short supply.

Costs to consider:

  • removing some en-suites or kitchen fittings
  • redecorating to a family-home style
  • a period with no rent while tenants leave and works happen

Large sui generis HMOs may need planning permission to change back, though councils often support returning a house to family use.

5. Sell to a housing provider or for supported living

Some housing associations, charities and supported living providers buy or lease shared houses. This market varies a lot by area and needs careful checking of the provider and terms.

6. Pass it on

Some investors plan to pass properties to their children. How you own the property, personally or through a company, makes a big difference to inheritance tax and capital gains tax. This is a specialist area, so get advice early. See buying through a limited company.

Tax on selling

If you own personally, capital gains tax applies to the gain on a residential property sale. Gains must be reported and the tax paid within 60 days of completion. Companies pay corporation tax on gains. Allow for this when planning an exit.

Keeping your options open

  • Get the paperwork right: planning, licensing and building control certificates.
  • Design flexibly: layouts that could work as a family home with modest changes.
  • Keep records: rents, bills and maintenance history.
  • Maintain the property: a well-kept house sells faster in any market.
  • Don't over-borrow: equity gives you choices.
